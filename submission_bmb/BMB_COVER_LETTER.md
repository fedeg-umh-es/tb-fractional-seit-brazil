Dear Editor-in-Chief,

Please consider our manuscript, **"Within-Family Error Reduction Versus External Forecasting Skill in Fractional-Order Compartmental Models: A Methodological Investigation of SEIT Tuberculosis Dynamics,"** for publication as an **Original Research** article in the *Bulletin of Mathematical Biology*.

The manuscript addresses a methodological evaluation question in mathematical epidemiology: when a fractional-order epidemic model reduces error relative to its integer-order counterpart, does that within-family improvement remain favorable when forecasting performance is assessed against external statistical and naive references under a temporal out-of-sample protocol?

We examine this question through an independent reimplementation of a Caputo fractional-order SEIT model using monthly tuberculosis surveillance data from Brazil (2001–2022). The fractional and integer-order SEIT models are fitted independently and evaluated using 13 expanding-window forecast origins across 12 monthly lead horizons, together with persistence, seasonal naive, and SARIMA benchmarks. The fractional formulation consistently reduces forecast error relative to the independently refitted integer-order comparator. However, that favorable within-family conclusion changes when the benchmark set is expanded: the fractional SEIT model shows no positive observed forecast skill relative to persistence or SARIMA at any evaluated horizon, while positive skill relative to seasonal naive is confined to horizons 1–7. Practical-identifiability analysis is included as a secondary interpretive axis, showing weak identification of several individual rate parameters despite comparatively stable predictions and a narrow practical-identifiability envelope for the derived basic reproduction number.

The methodological implication is deliberately bounded by this case study: **within-family error reduction is insufficient, by itself, to establish predictive utility. A forecasting claim for an added mechanistic flexibility should also be examined against appropriate external baselines under an explicit temporal out-of-sample protocol.** The manuscript does not infer a universal performance ranking for fractional models from this single empirical setting.

We believe this question is aligned with the *Bulletin*'s interest in work at the interface of mathematical methods and biological investigation. The journal has previously published work scrutinizing fractional derivatives in epidemiological models (Angstmann, Henry, and McGann, 2016, *A Fractional Order Recovery SIR Model from a Stochastic Process*) and, more recently, work on prediction under poor parameter identification (Simpson and Maclaren, 2024, *Making Predictions Using Poorly Identified Mathematical Models*). Our manuscript engages that methodological tradition empirically: it does not claim generic fractional superiority or theoretical novelty, but tests how the interpretation of a favorable fractional-versus-integer result changes under broader forecasting benchmarking.
